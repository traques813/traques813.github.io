Unit 5 extended the material from earlier weeks by connecting data-cleaning techniques with more realistic investigative scenarios. This unit positioned data wrangling not as an isolated technical step but as part of a larger analytical workflow grounded in source validation, reproducibility, and early exploration. These themes align with wider literature, where the reliability of downstream analysis depends heavily on the soundness of early-stage data preparation (Zumel & Mount, 2014).

A central component of the unit was the use of real-world case studies drawn from Chapter 6 of the textbook, including datasets relating to the Ebola outbreak in West Africa. These examples demonstrated how analytical work depends not only on having “clean” data but also on the ability to trace values back to their origin. As Gitelman (2013) argues, all data is “made” and carries a history that influences its interpretation. The case studies illustrated this point: missing documentation, inconsistent update cycles and unclear provenance created meaningful analytical constraints.

The UNICEF household survey data continued to serve as a practical dataset. This allowed earlier cleaning steps—such as header alignment, missing-value standardisation and type fixing—to be contextualised within investigative questions. Instead of treating cleaning as an isolated repair process, the unit framed it as a way to evaluate whether the dataset supported particular questions, a view consistent with the idea that data wrangling sits at the intersection of technical work and epistemic judgement (D’Ignazio & Klein, 2020).

The unit also introduced early automation concepts. Many cleaning tasks—format conversion, header replacement, missing-value handling—are repeated each time updated versions of similar datasets are released. This reflects broader DataOps principles, where reproducibility, scriptability and modular workflow design help reduce manual overhead and increase long-term reliability (Bogdanova et al., 2020). The unit’s emphasis on predictable, repeatable steps demonstrated how even simple scripts can form the backbone of more mature automated pipelines.

Early exploratory analysis was supported through the *agate* Python library. This lightweight toolset enabled type definitions, aggregations, sorting, ranking and summary statistics—activities that form the “profiling” stage of the data workflow. These exploratory actions help identify anomalies, understand initial distributions and shape the questions posed in later analysis. This aligns with Wickham’s (2014) argument that early exploration is essential for structuring analytical inquiry and recognising structural issues that may otherwise remain hidden.

Overall, Unit 5 reinforced that effective data wrangling is not solely a technical exercise but an interpretive one. The combination of case-based reasoning, structured cleaning, early automation and exploratory profiling created a more coherent view of how cleaned data supports meaningful analytical work. The unit emphasised that insight depends on both the *quality* of the data and the *process* used to transform it into a form suitable for interpretation.
