Unit 6 shifted the focus from file-based cleansing workflows towards **structuring cleaned data into relational databases**. The key theme across the unit was how database modelling transforms prepared datasets into systems that support reliable storage, querying and long-term maintenance. This link between cleaning work and schema design reflects a standard data engineering principle: analytical reliability depends as much on the structure of storage systems as on preprocessing techniques (Connolly & Begg, 2015).

The concept of **data normalisation** formed the core of this unit. Normalisation is a design technique that organises data into multiple related tables to eliminate redundancy and dependency anomalies while improving consistency (Date, 2013). Students worked through the progression from Unnormalised Form (UNF) to First, Second and Third Normal Forms (1NF–3NF), learning how each stage removes a distinct category of structural dependency. Functional dependencies, partial dependencies and transitive dependencies were each examined alongside the practical problems they create, such as update anomalies and inconsistent duplication.

This unit also linked directly to the group project: the development of an **Electricity Bill Management System (EBMS)**. Concepts from the module were applied to design tables covering customers, meters, meter readings, billing records, payment transactions and staff roles. **Primary keys and foreign keys** were used to enforce referential integrity between tables, ensuring that records could not exist without valid relational links (Elmasri & Navathe, 2016). This marked an important shift from earlier file-based handling to database-centred system thinking, where rule enforcement is delegated to the DBMS rather than manual processes.

An important realisation from this week was how **normalisation complements earlier cleaning work**. Tasks undertaken in previous units—such as removing duplicates, standardising fields and validating types—directly supported database design. Without these preparatory steps, enforcing table constraints becomes problematic. As Harrington (2016) notes, normalisation is effective only once input data has already achieved baseline consistency.

Tutor feedback on the project report — received during this stage — helped clarify how communication is as critical as technical modelling. The report presented justification for the chosen relational database before fully explaining the logical schema, which reduced narrative coherence. The structure conflicted with the recommended design sequence: conceptual model → logical schema → keys and constraints → normalisation → implementation. Revisiting the lecture material made clear how essential this sequence is when documenting database projects, ensuring that design decisions are logically justified rather than retroactively explained.

Another weakness identified in feedback was the limited explanation of the **data pipeline between raw collection and database staging**. Revisiting unit materials supported more reflective evaluation of this gap. Topics such as data staging tables, validation checkpoints and outlier handling had not been explained with sufficient relation to the EBMS use case. Building this understanding strengthened awareness that professional systems depend not just on final schemas but on **controlled transitional layers** that validate input before insertion (Kimball & Ross, 2013).

Overall, Unit 6 reinforced database modelling as a dual technical and communicative practice. Normalisation techniques were conceptually straightforward, but their value depended on the clarity with which they were documented and justified. The unit clearly demonstrated how cleaned datasets alone are not sufficient for scalable system design; robust schema construction and constraint enforcement are equally necessary to preserve integrity, consistency and auditability within operational environments.
